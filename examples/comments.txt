Overall a very interesting paper, but some things I think could be better

1) The recursion relation that leads to algorithm 3.2 seems to be a
   variation of the Clenshaw summation formula. No mention is made of that
   fact.
   
2) Pseudo code could be a better
    1) Use of the same variable name "i" for both a scalar variable
       and a vector variable and this can be confusing
    2) Index of a vector variable "i" starts at 1, while that of a
       matrix variable "r" starts at 0.  Again, confusing.

3) The algorithm for degree reduction (Algorithm 3.3) seems to be a varient
   of a standard technique of regularizing by throwing away sigular values /
   singular vectors whose singular value is close to zero.  No mention is
   made of that fact.

4) The authors imply that zeros in q(x) lead to "spurious" sigularities in
   the rational approximation r(x) = p(x) / q(x).  This is not true in general,
   since if the numerator p(x) has a zero of the same degree or higher than
   that of q(x) there is no pole.  Indeed, I replicated the experiment of
   using 1000 random points in [-1, 1] x [-1, 1] to fit the fuction
   f(x) = exp(x*y) / ( (x^2 - 1.44) * (y^2 - 1.44)) with polynomials up
   to degree (20, 20) and while there are interesting patterns of zeros
   in q(x) they seem to be always matched by zeros in p(x) (a couple of
   dozen runs with random sampling points). While it may be true that this
   could still pose some numerical challenges for optimization software,
   I think that would need to be investigated and reported.

   Now, it may be true that even if the zeros of p(x) and q(x) match, and
   are of the same degree, then the derivative of r(x) can have poles at
   the zeros of q(x).  You do need accurate derivative for optimization,
   so this could be a problem.

   Also, for these experiments, I always got singular values for the Z
   matrix ( $V_{M-1}^* F V_{N}$ ) that were only about an order of
   magnitude apart so that Algorithm 3.3 (Degree Reduction) could never
   reduce the degree below (20, 20).  However, if I manually reset the
   degree to (12, 9) I could reproduce the results of Figure 1.

Finally looking at the (20,20) approximation for exp(x*y)/((x^2 -
1.44)*(y^2 - 1.44)) for every random sampling of 1000 (x,y) points in
[-1,1]x[-1,1] the zeros of the denominator were matched by zeros of
the numerator inside of [-1.2,1.2]x[-1.2,1.2] I did not find a single
case where there was an actual spurious pole.  I must have generated
dozens of them, too. Obviously, there is something else going on here
but I do not know what.
